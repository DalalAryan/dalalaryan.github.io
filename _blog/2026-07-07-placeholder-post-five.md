Machine Learning sounds fancy, but what does a Machine Learning Researcher & Engineer actually do—no less at [NASA]()? There are several methods of engaging with ML at the forefront of the AI revolution and they differ greatly in the kind of industry your work sits at. At NASA, AI is less of a frontier research endeavor and more of a, “how can we leverage AI to solve some tough problems that involve the unpredictability and challenges of space environments?”. Research and engineering at NASA is not just designing autonomous rovers or sending rockets into outer-space—although that’s ultimately what we are known for. We are developing photovoltaics, designing highly resilient electronics, capturing deep space images, improving satellite communications technologies, understanding the nature of elementary particles and exploring science across all frontiers. Machine Learning, as such, is not a standalone pursuit but rather, a tool to support existing work and to improve technology across.

Perhaps it’s best for me to illustrate how machine learning interacts with scientific discovery and engineering through my own work\*. [Mitigating Arc Inception from Transformational Array Instrumentation]() is one of two main projects I am working on at NASA. Spacecraft charging is the excess accumulation of electrical charge as a result of operation in high energy environments (plasma, solar radiation/flares or cosmic rays) which can result in dangerous interaction with on-board electronics for scientific measurements. In particular, they can result in primary arcing and secondary arcing phenomenon. Detection of these arcs is fairly simple but predicting the rate of arcing into the future is where classical machine learning can help inform on-board systems to prepare for operation in a dangerous environment. What makes this challenging is that all of this occurs on the order of a couple of nanoseconds or microseconds. As a result, a model’s inference time has to be *rapid* fast if deployed on a FPGA/ASIC and needs to make precise predictions in a memory space of no more than a few kilobytes given the nature of embedded deployment. Many find it hard to believe that constraint-heavy engineering with sparse compute often resort to maximizing classical machine learning tools given the compute heavy requirements of major deep learning counterparts. Why use a 1D convolutional neural network when a simple logistic regression classifier can do the trick? Of course in projects where compute is not a constraint and where depth can truly benefit inference, deep learning excels wherein classical machine learning can no longer provide sufficient capacity for predictive or classification tasks. As I continue to work on this project, the more I realize how important a problem-solving mindset is to figuring out what works given the constraints. Having to account for a dynamic space environment, tiny compute, extreme solar radiation and other unpredictable factors, building a robust, accurate and rapid machine learning model is not just a task of using the best tools but understanding what design choices answer your requirements and where compromises might be required. 

[Circuit Optimization]() is the second project I have been working on at NASA—although fairly recently. LTSpice, Ngspice, Qspice are some of several circuit simulation tools available to build and analyze complex circuits. One of many challenges associated with a SPICE simulation tool is that as circuits become increasingly complex, the time it takes to simulate them can be on the order of multiple hours. In particular, under the hood of a SPICE tool is compiled C code that solves multiple differential equations in an attempt to parse through the nonlinear behavior of such circuits. As a result, naturally circuit optimization is a natural candidate for some fancy machine learning. Not just any kind of machine learning, this time we can perform some actual *deep learning*! Perhaps you may be familiar with the theory of multi-head attention in transformer models for large language models (LLMs) but have you thought of applying multi-head attention to nodes of a Graph Neural Network (GNN) or a Fourier Neural Operator with embedded physics to encode the graphical structure of circuits with randomized network topologies? I know, that’s a lot of jargon but inherently, these are the kind of ideas that are thrown around on the drawing board which can be seen as a stark contrast to the project I illustrated above where the requirement was to satisfy constraints rather than employing the frontier of machine learning research. What makes this project fun for me is that not only do I have experience using LTSpice to simulate circuits but I had never thought of applying deep learning to approach problems relating to circuit optimization. In hindsight, there’s so much potential for deep learning across niche industries such as electronic design automation which can cut simulation time by several orders with the power of deep learning methods to learn non-trivial functional mappings. 

One of the many intellectual pleasures of working at NASA is that you get to interact with people arriving from different expertise than you and one can be absolutely certain that they’re just as passionate about their field of interest as are you. From interacting with physicists and silicon engineers to visiting vacuum chamber deep space simulation test facilities, the boredom of a government agency is partially subdued from the amazing science you get to be a part of.
